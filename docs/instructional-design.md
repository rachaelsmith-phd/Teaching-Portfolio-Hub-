# Course Design

## Overview

My approach to course design combines instructional design, project management, curriculum development, and continuous improvement.

Over the course of my career, I have designed and revised courses across higher education, workforce development, public service training, and online learning environments. These experiences have reinforced a simple lesson: successful course design requires both structure and flexibility.

Every course begins with planning, but effective courses emerge through cycles of prototyping, testing, feedback, revision, and refinement.

For this reason, my design practice is informed primarily by the Successive Approximation Model (SAM), Agile design principles, and iterative development methods described by Cennamo and Kalk (2019).

---

# Instructional Design as Project Management

My approach to instructional design is informed not only by educational theory but also by professional experiences in legal services, criminal justice, child welfare, and public administration.

Prior to entering instructional design, I worked as a paralegal on complex white-collar crime, bankruptcy, tax fraud, crimes-against-children, dependency, guardianship, and parental severance cases. These environments required intensive project management, document control, deadline tracking, evidence organization, and coordination among multiple stakeholders operating within strict procedural frameworks.

Later, my work in child welfare, court advocacy, workforce development, and public service further reinforced the importance of structured planning, documentation, communication, and accountability.

As a result, I often approach course design much like a complex case-management project.

Before development begins, I establish:

- Project scope
- Stakeholder responsibilities
- Milestones and deliverables
- Dependencies
- Review cycles
- Accessibility checkpoints
- Launch schedules

Detailed Gantt charts frequently serve as project management tools throughout the development process. These planning documents help organize tasks, maintain accountability, monitor progress, and ensure that large curriculum projects remain aligned with institutional goals and timelines.

This structured planning process provides the foundation for a more iterative and flexible instructional design methodology.

The result is a design process that balances careful organization with continuous improvement, allowing creativity and innovation to occur within a well-managed development framework.

# Design Methodology

While project management provides the organizational structure for development, the learning experience itself is developed using iterative instructional design principles.

My instructional design practice is primarily informed by the Successive Approximation Model (SAM) and Agile design methodologies. These approaches recognize that effective learning experiences rarely emerge from a single design cycle. Instead, courses evolve through repeated cycles of development, review, testing, feedback, and refinement.

The combination of structured project management and iterative design allows complex courses to remain both organized and adaptable.

---

# Project Management

Before development begins, each project is organized using a detailed project management plan.

I rely heavily on Gantt charts and milestone tracking to establish:

- Project scope
- Stakeholder responsibilities
- Development timelines
- Review cycles
- Accessibility checkpoints
- Launch targets

This process allows complex projects to remain organized while maintaining flexibility throughout development.

Typical project milestones include:

1. Discovery and Analysis
2. Prototype Development
3. Internal Review
4. Revision Cycle
5. Quality Assurance
6. Course Launch
7. Continuous Improvement

Large projects often involve multiple overlapping review cycles before deployment.

# Three Development Phases

Before a course is considered complete, it progresses through three major phases.

## Phase I: Foundation

During the Foundation phase, the course architecture is established.

Focus areas include:

- Learning outcomes
- Module organization
- Course navigation
- Content inventory
- Assessment planning
- Accessibility planning

At this stage the course structure exists, but the learning experience is incomplete.

The objective is to create the framework upon which future development will occur.

---

## Phase II: Development

During the Development phase, content begins moving into a functional prototype.

Activities include:

- Canvas shell construction
- Assignment development
- Rubric development
- Media integration
- Learning activities
- Discussions
- Assessments
- Interactive content

This is often the most intensive phase of development.

Multiple revisions typically occur before a stable prototype emerges.

---

## Phase III: Refinement

The Refinement phase focuses on course quality, learner experience, accessibility, and usability.

This phase includes:

- Accessibility review
- Navigation testing
- Student workflow analysis
- Quality assurance
- Peer review
- Stakeholder feedback
- Course polishing

The goal is to move beyond content completion and create a coherent learning experience.

---

# Prototype Development

My design process relies heavily on iterative prototyping.

Rather than waiting until a course is complete before evaluation, development occurs through multiple versions.

---

## Prototype A

Prototype A establishes the initial learning environment.

Focus areas include:

- Basic course architecture
- Learning pathways
- Assignment structure
- Initial content
- Navigation framework

Prototype A is intentionally incomplete. Its purpose is to identify design challenges early in the process.

Questions include:

- Does navigation make sense?
- Are learning activities aligned?
- Are instructions clear?
- Are technological barriers present?

---

## Prototype B

Prototype B incorporates revisions generated through review and testing.

At this stage:

- Content becomes more robust
- Assessments become refined
- Accessibility issues are addressed
- Interactivity is expanded
- Learning pathways are clarified

Prototype B should closely resemble the final learner experience.

---

# Getting to Gold Build

A course reaches "Gold" status when it has completed all major design, review, accessibility, and quality assurance requirements.

Characteristics of a Gold build include:

- Complete content development
- Assessment alignment
- Accessibility compliance
- Functional navigation
- Quality assurance review
- Stakeholder approval
- Launch readiness

Gold status does not mean the course is permanently finished.

Rather, it indicates that the course has achieved a high level of readiness for implementation.

---

# Launch and Continuous Improvement

Course launch is not the end of the design process.

Consistent with SAM and Agile methodologies, implementation generates new information that informs future revisions.

Following launch, I review:

- Student feedback
- Learning analytics
- Assessment outcomes
- Accessibility concerns
- Faculty observations
- Emerging technologies

These findings inform future versions of the course.

In practice, course design becomes a cycle of continuous improvement rather than a one-time event.

# Example: Interactive Criminological Theory Design

One example of this design approach involved the development of an interactive Canvas learning experience for criminological theory.

Rather than presenting theories through traditional lectures alone, students navigated a branching "choose your own path" learning experience in which a single case could be interpreted using multiple theoretical perspectives.

Students explored:

- Classical Theory
- Strain Theory
- Social Learning Theory
- Control Theory
- Labeling Theory
- Routine Activities Theory

By making decisions and moving through different pathways, students experienced how theoretical assumptions shape interpretations of crime and behavior.

This project illustrates how iterative design, learner engagement, and technology integration can transform abstract concepts into meaningful learning experiences.

# Design Principles

Several principles guide all course development efforts.

### Courses are designed as experiences, not repositories.

### Accessibility is integrated from the beginning.

### Learning activities should reflect authentic professional practice.

### Technology should support learning rather than distract from it.

### Feedback should inform ongoing improvement.

### Effective design is iterative, collaborative, and continuously evolving.
