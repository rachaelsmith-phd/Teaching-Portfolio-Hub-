My teaching experience spans criminal justice, criminology, legal studies, victim advocacy, ethics, courts, corrections, policing, and emerging issues in technology and society. # Signature Graduate Seminar

## Victimology in the AI Era: Coercive Control, Digital Harm, and Survivor Reconstruction

This seminar represents the convergence of my academic preparation in Criminal Justice and Curriculum & Instruction, along with more than two decades of professional experience in legal services, child welfare, victim advocacy, criminal justice education, and instructional design.

The course examines how emerging technologies reshape contemporary experiences of victimization and justice. While grounded in feminist victimology, the seminar extends traditional approaches by exploring technology-facilitated abuse, coercive control, cyberstalking, digital surveillance, image-based exploitation, algorithmic harms, artificial intelligence, and survivor-centered approaches to recovery and reconstruction.

Key topics include:

- Techno-Victimology
- Coercive control
- Technology-facilitated abuse
- Cyberstalking and digital surveillance
- Online harassment and digital violence
- Artificial intelligence and victimization
- Child exploitation and online safety
- Trauma-informed practice
- Survivor-centered justice
- Human agency in technological environments

A central theme throughout the seminar is survivor reconstruction: the process through which individuals reclaim identity, agency, community, and meaning following victimization in increasingly digital environments.

More broadly, the course reflects my scholarly interest in the intersection of justice, technology, education, professional identity, and human agency in the twenty-first century.

# Signature Learning Experiences

Across courses, institutions, and professional training environments, I intentionally design learning experiences that connect theory, practice, reflection, and professional application.

Whether teaching criminal justice, legal studies, adult education, workforce development, or instructional design, my goal is to help learners move beyond content mastery toward meaningful understanding, critical inquiry, and professional competence.

Representative examples include:

---

### Criminological Theory Simulations

Interactive Canvas-based "choose your own pathway" learning experiences that allow students to analyze a single case through multiple criminological perspectives.

These simulations encourage students to explore how theoretical assumptions shape professional interpretations of crime, victimization, deviance, and justice.

---

### Case-Based Learning

Analysis of real-world criminal justice, legal, policy, and ethical dilemmas drawn from contemporary practice.

Students examine competing perspectives, evaluate evidence, and develop recommendations grounded in research, law, and professional standards.

---

### Professional Identity Reflection

Structured reflective assignments designed to help students examine professional values, ethical responsibilities, leadership practices, and career development.

These activities are particularly informed by my research on criminal justice educator identity and professional identity development.

---

### Community and Policy Analysis

Projects requiring students to evaluate criminal justice issues within broader social, political, environmental, and organizational contexts.

Students investigate how policy decisions affect individuals, communities, and public institutions.

---

### Technology and AI Integration

Assignments encouraging students to critically evaluate artificial intelligence, automation, digital technologies, and data systems within professional settings.

Students examine both opportunities and ethical challenges associated with emerging technologies.

---

### Mock Case File Development

Drawing upon my professional experience as a paralegal and legal assistant, students construct case files using authentic legal documentation practices.

Activities may include:

- Evidence organization
- Case chronology development
- Witness analysis
- Legal research
- Document preparation
- Professional writing

These assignments emphasize attention to detail, organization, and procedural accuracy.

---

### Legal Research and Writing Projects

Developed within paralegal studies and legal studies courses, students learn how to locate, interpret, analyze, and communicate legal information.

Projects emphasize:

- Legal reasoning
- Professional communication
- Citation and documentation
- Client-centered writing
- Research competency

---

### Adult Learning and Workforce Readiness Projects

Influenced by my work in workforce development and adult education, these assignments encourage learners to connect academic knowledge with professional goals.

Students engage in:

- Career pathway mapping
- Skills inventories
- Professional portfolio development
- Workforce competency analysis
- Professional development planning

---

### Scenario-Based Decision Making

Students navigate complex situations involving competing interests, incomplete information, and ethical uncertainty.

These activities mirror many of the realities faced by professionals in criminal justice, legal services, public administration, child welfare, and community organizations.

Rather than identifying a single correct answer, students learn to justify decisions using evidence, policy, ethics, and professional judgment.

---

### Survivor-Centered and Human Agency Projects

Drawing upon victimology, child advocacy, and social ecology perspectives, students explore how individuals and communities respond to adversity, victimization, and systemic challenges.

These projects emphasize:

- Human agency
- Resilience
- Recovery
- Community support
- Social responsibility

# Teaching Themes

Several themes consistently shape my teaching, curriculum development, instructional design, and scholarly work. While courses may differ in content, level, or institutional setting, these themes serve as an intellectual foundation that guides the learning experiences I create.

## Justice and Fairness

Students are encouraged to examine competing conceptions of justice, equity, accountability, and legitimacy. Criminal justice education should provide opportunities to critically evaluate systems while recognizing their impact on individuals, communities, and public institutions.

## Ethical Decision-Making

Professionals routinely encounter situations in which legal, organizational, and ethical responsibilities intersect. My courses encourage students to develop the habits of reflection, judgment, and moral reasoning necessary for responsible professional practice.

## Critical Thinking

Students should move beyond memorization toward analysis, evaluation, and synthesis. Learning experiences are designed to challenge assumptions, examine evidence, and engage complex questions from multiple perspectives.

## Evidence-Based Practice

Whether examining policy, practice, or organizational decision-making, students are encouraged to ground conclusions in evidence, research, and thoughtful analysis rather than opinion alone.

## Technology and Innovation

Technology continues to reshape criminal justice systems, education, communication, and public life. Students are encouraged to critically evaluate emerging technologies, including artificial intelligence, while considering their ethical, social, and professional implications.

## Professional Identity Development

A central goal of my teaching is to help students understand not only what professionals do, but who they are becoming as professionals. Professional identity development, reflective practice, communication, and lifelong growth are integrated throughout the curriculum.

## Civic Responsibility

Criminal justice is fundamentally public work. Students are encouraged to understand the relationship between professional practice, democratic institutions, public trust, and community well-being.

## Human Agency

While institutions, technologies, and organizations influence behavior, individuals retain the capacity to make choices, exercise judgment, and affect change. My teaching emphasizes the importance of human agency, responsibility, and leadership within complex systems.

## Community Engagement

Learning is strengthened when students connect classroom concepts to real-world communities and contemporary social issues. Courses frequently encourage students to consider the broader social, environmental, and organizational contexts in which justice operates.

## Lifelong Learning

Professional development does not end at graduation. Students are encouraged to cultivate curiosity, adaptability, reflection, and a commitment to continuous learning throughout their careers.

---

## Connecting Themes to Practice

Taken together, these themes reflect my belief that criminal justice education should prepare students to navigate complexity, engage emerging technologies responsibly, think critically, act ethically, and contribute meaningfully to their professions and communities.

These principles form the foundation of the **Unified Criminal Justice Curriculum Framework** and guide my approach to teaching across disciplines, institutions, and student populations.
